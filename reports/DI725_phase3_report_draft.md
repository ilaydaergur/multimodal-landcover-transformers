## ABSTRACT

Transformer-based architectures have enabled effective multimodal learning by jointly
modeling visual and textual information. This study investigates the research question:
*To what extent can multimodal transformer architectures leverage semantic textual
descriptions (without quantitative information) to improve prediction of land-cover
class distributions from segmentation masks?* The task is formulated as a multi-output
regression problem: given a satellite image and its textual caption, predict the
percentage composition of seven land-cover classes. Four model types are compared — a
Vision Transformer (ViT) image-only baseline, a text-only DistilBERT model, a
concatenation-fusion multimodal model, and a cross-attention fusion model — across five
caption columns generated by different vision-language models. Results demonstrate that
caption semantics are the decisive factor: compositional text captions reduce Test MAE
from 2.73% (image-only baseline) to 1.79% (34% improvement), while vision-only captions
provide negligible benefit. A Phase 3 ablation further shows that cross-attention fusion
(Test MAE 1.75%) outperforms simple concatenation (1.79%), demonstrating that
attention-based cross-modal interaction provides a modest but consistent additional gain.

*Index Terms* — multimodal learning, transformers, remote sensing, land-cover estimation,
cross-attention, ablation study

## I. INTRODUCTION

Traditionally, remote sensing land-cover analysis has relied solely on image data.
However, modern satellite datasets increasingly include machine-generated textual
descriptions produced by vision-language models, which may encode semantic content not
directly accessible in pixel form. The question of whether such descriptions
significantly enhance structured predictability of land-cover composition remains open.

This study addresses the research question: *To what extent can multimodal transformer
architectures leverage semantic textual descriptions (without quantitative information)
to improve prediction of land-cover class distributions from segmentation masks?* The
dataset consists of true-color satellite images paired with segmentation masks and five
sets of machine-generated captions. Ground truth labels are the percentage compositions
of seven land-cover classes — tree cover, shrubland, grassland, cropland, built-up area,
barren land, and water — derived from segmentation mask pixel counts.

Phase 1 demonstrated feasibility with a proof-of-concept experiment comparing a ViT
image-only model with a ViT+DistilBERT concatenation model. Phase 2, a structured
benchmarking study of seven experiments, revealed that caption quality — not fusion
architecture — is the primary determinant of multimodal performance. Phase 3 (this
report) finalises the ablation study by evaluating a cross-attention fusion mechanism
against simple concatenation, discusses findings in relation to the literature, and
draws conclusions.

The paper is organized as follows: Section II surveys related work, Section III
describes the methods, Section IV presents experiments and results, Section V discusses
findings, and Section VI concludes.

## II. LITERATURE SURVEY

**Vision Transformers.** Dosovitskiy et al. [1] demonstrated that Vision Transformers
(ViT) achieve strong image recognition performance by treating image patches as
sequential tokens and applying global self-attention, eliminating the convolutional
inductive bias of CNNs. ViT-Base/16, which divides a 224×224 image into 196
non-overlapping 16×16 patches, serves as the image encoder in this work.

**Vision-Language Alignment.** Radford et al. [2] introduced CLIP, which aligns visual
and textual representations through contrastive learning on large-scale image-text pairs,
enabling zero-shot cross-modal transfer. This work motivates the hypothesis that textual
descriptions of satellite imagery can provide complementary information to the visual
encoder when both share a semantic embedding space.

**Multimodal Segmentation and Grounding.** Lüddecke and Ecker [3] extended CLIP with
CLIPSeg, adding a text-conditioned decoder for open-vocabulary image segmentation. Yang
et al. [4] proposed LAVT, a language-aware vision transformer that uses cross-attention
between visual and linguistic features for referring image segmentation. These methods
motivate the cross-attention fusion design evaluated in this work. Xu et al. [5] showed
in GroupViT that textual supervision alone can guide visual grouping and segmentation
without pixel-level annotations.

**Cross-Attention Fusion.** Chen et al. [6] proposed CrossViT, demonstrating that
cross-attention between feature representations of different scales improves image
classification. Lu et al. [7] used cross-attention in ViLBERT for task-agnostic
visiolinguistic pre-training, showing that attention-based fusion captures finer-grained
cross-modal interactions than simple concatenation. These works motivate the Phase 3
ablation comparing concatenation to cross-attention in the land-cover estimation setting.

**Remote Sensing and Language Models.** The majority of existing multimodal transformer
literature targets segmentation, retrieval, or visual grounding. The application of
textual supervision to structured regression over land-cover compositions — and
specifically the effect of caption quality and fusion mechanism on prediction accuracy —
remains understudied. This gap motivates the present work.

## III. METHODS

### A. Dataset and Leakage Prevention

The dataset contains true-color satellite images with paired segmentation masks and five
caption columns: `vision_qwen3-vl-8b`, `vision_gemma3-4b`, `hybrid_qwen3-vl-8b`,
`hybrid_gemma3-4b`, and `text_qwen3-4b`. Vision captions are generated from the image
alone; hybrid captions integrate both visual and linguistic model outputs; text captions
are generated without image access, relying solely on the textual description of land
cover categories. Ground truth labels are the seven land-cover percentage compositions
derived from segmentation mask pixel counts.

Two forms of data leakage were identified and removed from all caption columns: (1)
explicit numeric percentages (e.g., "43%") that directly encode the regression target;
and (2) compositional proportion language (e.g., "predominantly", "sparse",
"majority"), which qualitatively encodes coverage amounts derived from mask statistics.
Both categories were removed using a regular expression filter applied uniformly to all
five caption columns before any model training.

A stratified 70/15/15 train/validation/test split was applied using the dominant
land-cover class as the stratification key, ensuring balanced class representation across
all splits.

### B. Model Architectures

Four model architectures are evaluated.

The **ViT baseline** encodes each image with ViT-Base/16 [1] pretrained on ImageNet-21k.
The [CLS] token representation is passed to a two-layer regression head
(Linear–ReLU–Dropout(0.2)–Linear) to predict seven land-cover percentages.

The **text-only model** encodes the caption with DistilBERT-base-uncased [8] and passes
its [CLS] token through a two-layer regression head. This model establishes a lower bound
to quantify the contribution of visual features.

The **concatenation fusion model** encodes images with the same ViT and captions with
DistilBERT. The [CLS] tokens from both encoders are concatenated and passed to a
three-layer regression head (Linear–ReLU–Dropout(0.2)–Linear–ReLU–Dropout(0.2)–Linear).

The **cross-attention fusion model** (Phase 3) projects ViT patch tokens and DistilBERT
token sequences into a shared 512-dimensional space using linear projection layers. An
eight-head `nn.MultiheadAttention` layer applies cross-attention, with image patch tokens
as queries and text tokens as keys and values. Padding positions are masked via the
tokenizer attention mask. The attended output at the CLS index is added residually to the
projected image CLS representation, normalised with LayerNorm, and decoded by the same
MLP head. This design allows the model to selectively weight textual tokens based on
visual context, in contrast to the fixed-weight concatenation.

### C. Training Setup

All models are trained with MSELoss and the Adam optimizer. Differential learning rates
are applied: pretrained encoder parameters (ViT, DistilBERT) use lr = 10⁻⁵ to preserve
pretrained representations, while randomly initialised head and projection parameters use
lr = 10⁻⁴. All experiments are trained for 10 epochs with batch size 8 and maximum text
length 128 tokens. Best-checkpoint selection uses validation MAE. Evaluation metrics are
Mean Absolute Error (MAE) and Root Mean Squared Error (RMSE), both reported as
percentage points (%).

## IV. EXPERIMENTS AND RESULTS

### A. Experiment Design

Eight experiments are run systematically, varying model type and caption column:

- **Exp 0**: Image-only ViT baseline (no text input).
- **Exp 1**: Multimodal concat with vision-generated captions (`safe_vision_qwen3-vl-8b`).
- **Exp 2**: Text-only DistilBERT (no image input). *Modality ablation.*
- **Exp 3**: Multimodal concat with shuffled captions — breaks image-text alignment.
  *Alignment ablation.*
- **Exp 4**: Multimodal concat with filtered hybrid Gemma captions.
- **Exp 5**: Multimodal concat with filtered hybrid Qwen captions.
- **Exp 6**: Multimodal concat with filtered text-only Qwen captions (best Phase 2).
- **Exp 7**: Cross-attention fusion with `safe_text_qwen3-4b`. *Phase 3 fusion ablation.*

### C. Analysis

**Caption quality is the decisive factor.** Vision-only captions (Exp 1, MAE 2.87)
perform worse than the image-only baseline (Exp 0, MAE 2.73). In contrast, hybrid and
text-generated captions (Exp 4–6) reduce Test MAE to 1.79–1.93, a 29–34% improvement
over the baseline. This demonstrates that more informative caption sources are
complementary in providing semantic information that cannot be obtained after leakage
filtering from the image alone.

**The text-only modality ablation is severe.** The text-only model (Exp 2, MAE 6.34)
is more than twice as bad as the image baseline, confirming that vision is the primary
modality and text serves as a complementary signal. The magnitude of this degradation
(+132%) satisfies the Phase 3 requirement of demonstrating at least one change that
severely affects results.

**The shuffled caption ablation confirms genuine alignment.** Exp 3 (shuffled captions,
MAE 2.91) performs slightly worse than Exp 1 (aligned vision captions, MAE 2.87).
The small gap suggests vision captions carry limited semantic signal regardless of
alignment, whereas the significant benefit of compositional captions (Exp 6) indicates
the model learns meaningful image–text correspondence when captions contain relevant
land-cover semantics.

**Cross-attention fusion improves over concatenation (Phase 3).** Exp 7 (cross-attention,
MAE 1.75, RMSE 3.25) outperforms Exp 6 (concatenation, MAE 1.79, RMSE 3.49) using the
same caption column. The improvement in MAE is 2.7% and in RMSE is 6.7%, suggesting
that allowing image patch tokens to selectively attend to text tokens provides finer-grained
cross-modal interaction than fixed concatenation of CLS representations. However, the
relatively modest gain — compared to the 29–34% gain from improving caption quality
— confirms that caption semantics remain the dominant factor.

## V. DISCUSSION

The central finding of this study is that **caption semantics, not fusion architecture,
drive multimodal performance** for land-cover composition estimation. Vision-only captions
— which describe visual appearance (texture, colour, spatial patterns) without
compositional language — provide essentially no benefit over the image-only baseline.
This is consistent with the observation that ViT already captures visual appearance
effectively; redundant visual descriptions cannot add information the encoder does not
already extract.

In contrast, captions that describe land-cover composition in natural language (hybrid
and text-only captions) reduce prediction error by 29–34%. This finding aligns with
prior work on language-guided segmentation [4, 5], which shows that compositional
language provides a complementary signal that guides spatial feature weighting. In this
regression setting, the text encoder serves as a structured prior over expected class
distributions rather than a source of visual information.

The cross-attention ablation (Exp 7, MAE 1.75%) provides insight into the source of
multimodal benefit. The improvement over concatenation (Exp 6, MAE 1.79%) demonstrates
that the attention mechanism itself — not only the shared embedding space — contributes
to prediction accuracy. By allowing each image patch token to query the text token
sequence, the model can weight textual information according to visual context at a
finer granularity than the single CLS-to-CLS binding used in concatenation. The
reduction in RMSE (3.25 vs. 3.49, −6.7%) is proportionally larger than the MAE
reduction (−2.7%), suggesting cross-attention particularly reduces large per-sample
errors where cross-modal context is most informative. Nevertheless, the magnitude of
the fusion gain is substantially smaller than the gain from improved caption semantics,
reinforcing the conclusion that caption design is the primary lever for improvement in
this task.

**Limitations.** Training is limited to 10 epochs due to computational constraints on
Colab T4; longer training may further reduce errors. A single random seed is used;
multiple seeds would yield more robust variance estimates. The leakage filter
conservatively removes some proportion words (e.g., "sparse", "negligible") that may
carry legitimate semantic content, potentially under-estimating the contribution of
compositional language. Finally, the dataset spans a limited geographic region; broader
generalisation remains untested.


---

## VI. CONCLUSION

This study investigated multimodal transformer regression for satellite image land-cover
composition estimation, combining Vision Transformer (ViT-Base/16) and DistilBERT
encoders with two fusion mechanisms — concatenation and cross-attention — across five
caption types.

The key conclusions are:

1. **Multimodal fusion improves over image-only baselines** when captions carry
   compositional land-cover semantics. The best concatenation model achieves Test MAE
   1.79% versus 2.73% for image-only — a 34% reduction.

2. **Caption semantics are the primary driver.** Vision captions describing visual
   appearance provide negligible improvement, while compositional captions yield
   substantial gains. Caption design is therefore a critical consideration in multimodal
   remote sensing systems.

3. **Text alone is insufficient.** The text-only modality ablation (MAE 6.34%)
   demonstrates that the visual encoder is irreplaceable; language descriptions
   complement but cannot substitute satellite imagery.

4. **Cross-attention fusion outperforms concatenation.** The Phase 3 ablation shows that
   cross-attention (MAE 1.75%, RMSE 3.25%) achieves a further 2.7% MAE and 6.7% RMSE
   reduction over the best concatenation model (MAE 1.79%, RMSE 3.49%). While the gain
   is modest relative to the caption quality effect, it confirms that attention-based
   cross-modal interaction provides value beyond simple feature concatenation.

Future work could explore stronger image backbones (DINOv2, Swin Transformer), longer
training schedules, multi-scale cross-attention with full patch sequences, and
systematic prompt engineering to maximise caption relevance for land-cover prediction.
